## 2. The premise and lore

### Three premises

**A. The Survey.** You're a contract pilot hired for one season by a tiny outfit at the end of the road. The outfit has a government contract: the interior was last surveyed from the air in the 1930s, the maps are wrong, and a university archaeologist wants the ruins marked on those old maps found and checked. The jobs are ordinary — mail, fuel, a pump for a station, a count for the ranger — and the ruins are what you keep finding on the way. They were built by a people who managed water across a dry land with channels, weirs and raised stone roads. The story builds to the one site no one has reached: a city the old maps put in the middle of the dunes. Tone: quiet, factual, National Geographic with an engine note.

**B. The Return.** The outfit belonged to your uncle, who died flying its plane. It's broke. You come out to sell it and instead take the jobs to keep it alive, and you find his logbook: a decade of strips he cut and ruins he found, and a theory about where the old channels lead. The story builds to finishing his last flight. Tone: personal, melancholy, a game about a place and a person.

**C. The Water.** The country is drying out. The old builders managed water; their works are why there's any water now. A resource company wants the aquifer under the dunes; the stations and the town need it; the ruins hold the map of where the water goes. You fly for the town and find yourself between the company and the archaeologist. Builds to a choice about what you tell whom. Tone: grounded thriller, a little political.

### The pick: A, with the water from C underneath it

A is the strongest because it's **true to bush flying**: the jobs are the jobs, and discovery happens because you're the only person who goes everywhere. It also gives the title ("Ground Truth"), the pacing (survey lines open the map), and a reason the ruins matter today (C's water), without needing a villain to carry it. B's logbook becomes the 1930s surveyor's notes (below) — a ghost in the story without a person to model.

**The land.** The town calls everything past the End "the Interior". It's a dry country the size of a small nation: savanna and creeks in the south, salt pans and a sea of dunes in the west, a cut sandstone canyon country in the east, a high cold tableland in the north behind a 600 m escarpment, a river that comes off that tableland and fans into a delta and a lake in the north-east, and a black volcanic range in the far north-west. Painted mountains in every direction beyond that. The one paved road, **Last Road**, comes up from the south coast and ends at Roadhead. Nothing else is sealed.

**The Old Hands.** Fifteen hundred years ago (the archaeologist says; the stations say "the old people") someone built a water system across the whole interior. They tapped the spring on the tableland, took the water over a weir at the top of the falls, ran it along a dead-straight raised stone causeway 30 km across the savanna, through stone cisterns and small temples, and — this is the mystery — **into the dunes**. The scholars call them the Channel Builders. The station families call them **the Old Hands**, because at every channel head there's the same carving: an open hand, palm out, the sign for "the water is held here". You put your gloved hand on it to read it.

**The mystery.** Why would anyone send water into a desert? Because the dunes weren't there. The city at the end of the channel was a lake city. The climate turned, the lake went, the sand came east and buried it. The channels still carry a trickle underground — that's why the Three Wells at the dune's edge have never gone dry, and why the aquifer the resource company wants is where it is. Nobody has stood in the city since the 1930s surveyor who marked it on his map and then crashed somewhere north of it.

**The present.** Meridian Resources has a drilling lease at the delta and a survey crew working toward the dunes. Dr Haugen has a small contract and a smaller budget to prove the city exists before the lease is extended over it. Dell has a plane, a truck, a bad knee and a season's worth of jobs. You have a licence.

**What it builds to.** Finding the Old Survey Strip from the air; the surveyor's camp and his notes; a night drive through the dune corridor in a dust storm because the plane is grounded; walking into the Sunken City and finding the cistern still holding water; and flying Haugen over it at dawn to put it on the map. No fight. The ending is a correct map and a lease that lapses. Then the whole country stays open for side jobs.

### Regions and how they connect

1. **The Savanna** (x 14–34, y 0–30, the south and centre). Dry gold grass, flat-topped trees, creek lines, granite boulder hills (kopjes), termite mound plains, a cattle station. The driving heart: open ground you can cross anywhere. The Old Hands' **Causeway** runs dead straight across it, south-west to north-east, from the dune's edge to the escarpment foot.
2. **The Salt Pans** (x 0–16, y 0–14, the south-west corner). Dead flat, white, 12 km across. Mirage, no reference for height, a strip marked by oil drums. The one place the truck can go flat out — a quiet nod to Redline.
3. **The Dune Sea** (x 0–18, y 14–40, the west). Star dunes to 300 m. Fly-over country: nowhere firm to land, and the truck sinks past the first line of dunes except along one corridor. The Sunken City is in the middle of it.
4. **The Highlands** (x 14–36, y 30–48, the north centre). A 600 m escarpment, climbed by one switchback pass, then a cold tableland at 1,900 m with the spring, the terraces, low cloud in the mornings and a thin band of cloud forest on the north face. The air is thinner: longer take-off rolls.
5. **The Canyons** (x 34–48, y 14–40, the east). Sandstone cut into mesas, slots, arches and hoodoos. The live river runs through the northern half in a gorge; the southern half is dry canyon that floods after rain.
6. **The Delta and the Lake** (x 36–48, y 38–48, the north-east). The river fans into reed channels and a lake. Birds, mist, soft ground, and Meridian's drill camp on a levee.
7. **The Black Range** (x 0–14, y 40–48, the north-west). Cinder cones, lava fields spilling onto the north edge of the dunes, a caldera with a lake in it, steam vents. Hard to get to; the far outstation sits where the lava meets the sand.

**Last Road** enters from the south edge at (22, 0), curves north-east for 14 km through the savanna fringe, passes the Kettle Creek turn-off, runs through Roadhead at (32, 7) and dies 2 km east at **the End**: a cattle grid, a sign, and tarmac that simply stops in the grass. Driving the 14 km of pavement is the only time the truck's tyres hum.

## 4. On foot

**Principle:** a walk site is a place, not a puzzle box. The satisfying part is arriving — the drive in, the walk up, the first look inside — and the finding. Keep the mechanics to a handful that each need almost no code.

**Controls (phone):** left thumb walks, right thumb looks, one **Use** button, a tool swipe. No jump, no crouch. On the laptop, WASD and mouse.

**The hands:** two gloved hands in view, one model, three poses (empty, holding, touching). The held tool is a separate baked model swapped into the right hand.

**The tools, and what each one is in code:**

| Tool | What you do | Code behind it |
|---|---|---|
| **Lantern** | Dark halls. Light what's in front of you. | A point light that moves with the camera; no shadows, baked shade does the work. |
| **Field book** | Opens like a map. Fills in by itself: every glyph, sketch and photo. | A page with slots that turn from blank to filled. |
| **Camera** | Frame a find and press. The field book gets the photo. | Copy a 256×256 of the screen into a texture when the reticle is on a marked find. |
| **Rope** | Clip to a ring at a drop, hold Use to go down. One way. | A scripted descent along a line; the way out is on foot through the site. This is the "find the way through". |
| **Machete** | Vines across a doorway. Three swings clear them. | A blocker with a hit count. |
| **Pry bar** | A stone cover, a jammed door. | A blocker that needs the tool. |
| **Rubbing kit** | Press paper on a carving: you get the glyph in the book. | Same as camera, but for glyphs; the hand pose "touching". |
| **Your hand** | On the open-hand carving: the channel head "reads" — a line of light runs along the channel to show where the water goes. | A short glow animation along a path. It's the story's compass. |

**What a site asks of you:** one of three things, sometimes two.
- **Find the thing** — a channel head, a map stone, a statue, a notebook — marked only when you're close and looking.
- **Open the way** — vines, a pried cover, a rope drop, a stair you have to find by following the channel.
- **Read the place** — collect three glyphs in the right order (they're carved along the channel's direction), and the book tells you what they say. Each site adds a few words to the Old Hands' alphabet; by the end you can read the City's wall.

**Length:** 5–8 minutes for a small site (the Cistern), 12–15 for a big one (the Terraces, the City). Never longer than the drive in.

**How sites tie in:** every main mission that has a site ends with a find that points to the next place (the Cistern's channel points west; the Map Stone shows the system; Venn's notebook gives the corridor). Side jobs reuse sites lightly: "photograph the Alcove carving for Haugen", "read the Petroglyph Wall for Ruben". Finds are also money: Haugen pays per photographed glyph.

**New code (be honest):** this is the biggest new thing in the game. It needs walking collision inside buildings (walls as simple invisible boxes, floors as the mesh), a camera at eye height, the hands, the tool swap, the Use prompt, and a way to enter and leave the truck. Interiors should be built as a few big rooms with baked stone surfaces — never corridors with many small parts. The sun's cascaded shadows don't help indoors; baked ambient shade plus the lantern do.

## 5. The Outfitter

The bay is Teo's: a lift, a bench, a wall of gear with weights chalked on it, the dyno, and the vehicle you're prepping. "A little technical" means: real numbers, a weight sheet, a range circle on the map, and consequences you can feel.

### Weight, in plain numbers

- **Plane:** empty 620 kg. Max take-off 1,050 kg. You weigh 85 kg with your kit. Full fuel (90 L) is 65 kg. That leaves **280 kg** for cargo. Over it, it still flies, but the roll gets longer, the climb gets lazier, the stall speed goes up, and the short strips stop being possible. Teo won't sign the sheet over 1,050 kg.
- **Truck:** payload 750 kg. Weight makes it slower in sand and on the climb, and heavier on the brakes downhill. Less critical than the plane, but you feel a 200 L drum in the back.

### The gear (about 30)

| Item | kg | What it does or unlocks |
|---|---|---|
| Sand ladders (pair) | 14 | Get out of soft sand. Needed for the Dune Corridor. |
| Shovel | 3 | Dig out. Halves the time stuck. |
| Tow strap and shackles | 5 | Recover the Pan Wreck and stranded trucks (jobs). |

### Preparing the truck

- **Tyre pressures** (the one he'll love): sand 15 psi, rock 25, track 30, tarmac 38. Low pressure in sand raises grip and lowers rolling drag; low pressure on tarmac heats the tyres and slows the truck. Each surface type already has grip and drag; pressure scales them. Shown as a gauge; the compressor lets you change it in the field.
- **Suspension lift** (+50 mm clearance, more roll in corners).
- **Snorkel** (wading depth 0.6 m → 1.0 m; opens the Gorge Bottom crossings).
- **Winch** (recover yourself from a tree or rock; required for the storm drive).
- **Light bar** (night driving; also lights a strip for the plane).
- **Long-range tank** (+90 L).
- **Bigger tyres** (33" → 35": clearance and sand flotation, slower off the line, taller gearing).
- **Rear locker** (the Switchback and the Needle).
- **Roof rack** (+150 kg payload, higher centre of gravity).
- **Dyno tuning** (as now: torque vs top speed; a sand tune and a highland tune).

### Preparing the plane

- **Tundra tyres** (rough and short strips; the Old Survey Strip needs them; −5 mph cruise).
- **Climb propeller or cruise propeller** (a real choice: climb prop shortens the take-off roll 25% and lowers cruise 7 mph; cruise prop the reverse). The tableland wants the climb prop.
- **Long-range tank** (+40 L).
- **Cargo pod** (above).
- **STOL flap kit** (stall speed down 3 mph; the Bar and the Caldera get easier).
- **Landing lights** (night strips).
- **Oil cooler** (hot afternoons at the pans and the rig; without it the engine temperature creeps and power drops).

### The pre-trip check (15 seconds, four taps, real)

1. **Weight and fuel sheet** — shows total weight against the limit and draws the **range circle on the map**. If the job's strip is outside the circle, you see it now.
2. **Walk-around** — the camera orbits the plane; a tap on each of three points (prop clear, tie-downs off, flaps set). Teo's line: "Tie-downs. You'd be surprised."
3. **Control check** — move the stick; the ailerons, elevators and rudder move. You watch them.
4. **Run-up** — throttle to the mark, the engine note changes, a tap on each magneto drops the revs. Then "Grasshopper, Roadhead, departing two-seven."

The truck's version is two taps: pressures set for the first surface, recovery gear aboard. After the first trip, a **Quick check** does it in one tap. It's a ritual, not a chore.

### Fuel — my recommendation (his call)

Yes for the plane, generous but real: it's what makes weight, range, the far outstation and the drum run mean anything. For the truck, mostly yes-but-invisible: a long-range tank makes it a non-issue except in the dunes, where running dry is the risk you plan around. A fuel bowser in town and drums at the far strips.

### Pacing: money, gear, the map

- **The map is open to fly from the start.** Nothing stops you flying to the Black Range on day one. The first three mods come from the story's own jobs; the rest from side work. Roughly: ten side jobs per big mod, which is the right amount of "travel between places".
- **Fuel** costs money (if he says yes), which gives the side jobs a reason that never runs out.

## 7. Life and atmosphere

### Distant animals (cheap, and true: they run before you arrive)

Everything is one draw per kind, instanced, 150–400 triangles each, legs swung by a sine in the vertex shader (no skeleton). Nothing is drawn inside 120 m of the camera; inside 300 m the herd's flow is pushed away from you, so you only ever see rumps and dust. Species to pick with him; my suggestions:

| Where | What | How it's drawn |
|---|---|---|
| Savanna | **Antelope herds** (30–80), **cattle** near the station, **ostrich-like runners** in twos | Instanced, 300 tris, two-frame leg swing; dust puffs under the herd. |
| Dune edge and pans | **Oryx-type** in small lines along dune crests (a classic silhouette), **wild camels** on the Caravan | 300 tris; placed on crests by formula so they're backlit at sunset. |
| Pans, wet | **Pink birds** in sheets | Instanced quads; take off as a cloud when the plane passes. |
| Delta and lake | **Cranes** lifting from the reeds, **hippo backs** (humps only, never a whole animal) | Quads with wing-flap sine; humps are 40 tris each. |
| Everywhere high | **Vultures** circling | A dozen quads in a slow spiral — and the spiral marks a thermal the plane can use. |
| The Marbles, the Rim | **One big animal on a ridge** at dawn and dusk | A single 600-tri model placed on a skyline rock; it walks off when you're within 200 m. Never closer. |
| Night | **Eyes** in the headlights | Two bright quads at ground level that blink out. |

### Sounds

- **Birdsong by region and time:** savanna doves at dawn, cicadas in the heat, delta cranes, nothing but wind in the dunes, a single bird call on the tableland in cloud.
- **Rustling as something runs off** when you walk near bush: a one-shot sound 10–30 m away, with the bush's grass cards shaking for a second. Cheap and uncanny.
- **Wind** by altitude, speed and region; the wires sing over 90 mph.
- **The radio:** Flight Service's weather every quarter hour, Dell's calls, strip chatter when you're within range, static when you're not.
- **The truck's tyres** already change with surface; add the hum on the tarmac and the crunch on salt.
- **The hand on the carving:** a low tone that runs away along the channel.

### Weather

- **Dust storms** (the big one): Flight Service warns three hours ahead; a brown wall panorama slides in from the west, visibility drops to 200 m, the plane is grounded, sand ticks across the screen. The storm mission uses it; otherwise once a week of game time.
- **Rain on the savanna:** short, heavy, the creeks run for a day, the Bar is under water, the pans get their sheet of pink birds. Already have spray and mud.
- **Heat haze** over the pans and the lava: a cheap screen wobble near the ground at noon.
- **Low cloud on the highlands:** a fog band between 1,800 and 2,100 m until mid-morning; the Terrace Strip is in it. Flying up through it to clear sky is one of the game's moments.
- **Delta mist** at dawn, gone by eight.

### Times of day and night

The engine has them. Night flying is a real feature: Roadhead's lights, the rig's lights at 20 km, the stars, and a dirt strip lit by one truck. A night mail run to the station should be an early side job, before the storm mission needs the light bar.

Two honest notes on pictures:

- **Graphics memory.** A 2048 picture is 22 MB. Two vehicles with three 2048 maps each is 130 MB. Twelve walk sites at 1024 ×2 is 130 MB more. Plus terrain and trees. That is near what a phone browser will give. So: **a site's pictures must only live in graphics memory while you're within about 2 km of it**, and be thrown away after. One file can still do that: keep the data as text in the page and only make the texture when needed.
- **File size.** The plane added 6 MB. Normal maps don't compress well. Expect 4–7 MB per baked walk site, so twelve sites is 60–80 MB on top of the vehicles and textures. A 150 MB page loads on a Pixel 7a, but slowly, and every rebuild is that size. Keep landmark normal maps at 512 where they can be; most carved stone reads fine.

A point about the Outfitter models, offered gently because his rule is binding: the source models are **code**, which costs almost nothing in the file, while a 150k bake costs 6 MB per vehicle. The bay is a closed room with only the vehicle in it, so the 790k Studio 2 would fit the phone's budget there as it does in Salt & Sand today. If the look test shows a 150k bake looking worse than the source up close, the bay could show the source instead and save 12 MB and a lot of baking work. His call; I just want it on the table.

## 10. Risks and cuts

### What's most likely to go wrong

1. **The play models look worse than the Studio cars up close.** He's already seen this at 51k. The outline is what fails: tyres go faceted, struts and wires go flat. The bake must keep tread, bolts, struts and wires as real geometry and spend the 10k there, not on the body panels (which bake beautifully). If the look test fails at 10k, 25k is probably the honest answer and still leaves 800k for the world.

2. **Graphics memory and file size.** Said above. The fix is loading a site's pictures only when near, halving landmark normal maps, and watching the page size every build. Set a line: the page stays under 150 MB, or something gets cut.

3. **First person is all new code.** Collision, interiors, hands, tools, entering and leaving the truck. It will take longer than any single step in the build order. Build the Cistern first as a one-room test before any other site is modelled.

4. **The AI helpers build bad geometry.** They can't see their own output. The render-and-fix loop works but is slow per model; a temple at 50k triangles may need ten rounds. Start helpers on gear and small stones (easy, clear reference) before temples.

5. **"Something new every 30–60 seconds" depends on the generator**, not the landmarks. If the generated filler (creeks, kopjes, mounds, windmills, herds) is thin, flight is boring no matter how good the fifty places are. Test flight boredom early with no landmarks at all.

6. **The plane's weight needs to matter in the flight model.** Check that mass is a real input to the lift, roll and climb; if the racing game's plane has a fixed mass, the whole weight sheet is theatre until that's changed.

7. **Shadows and lights indoors.** Cascaded sun shadows do nothing inside; the lantern needs baked shade on the walls to look like anything. Bake ambient shade into every interior.

8. **Phone screen clutter.** The on-foot screen (stick, look, Use, tool swipe) and the bay (sliders, sheets) both risk it. Every element on the phone needs a reason.

9. **Two vehicles at a strip.** The truck waiting beside the runway while the plane is active is two physics bodies and two models near the camera; the robot drivers prove multiple vehicles work, but the hand-off (park one, wake the other, save which is where) is a save-file question. Keep "the plane stays parked" simple: it's a static model until you walk back to it.

10. **The storm drive.** Night + dust + winch + the dune corridor is the hardest mission to make feel good on a phone (visibility, lighting, stuck logic). Prototype the dust alone early.

### What to cut first if time or budget runs short, in order

1. **The Black Range** region (the caldera, the vents, the Observatory Cone, the Lava Track). Move Last Well to the north-west dune edge. The map keeps its size; the corner becomes fly-over dunes and painted mountains.
2. **The Delta**: fold the count and the Meridian camp onto the lake shore at the end of the Gorge; one strip fewer.
3. **The rope drops**: the Alcove gets a cliff stair instead; the City's stair is just a stair.
4. **The camera as a feature**: looking at a marked find logs it; no snapshot code.
5. **Weather down to dust storms and highland cloud**; no rain.
6. **The Outfitter's 150k models**: show 50k bakes, or the source in code (see section 8).
7. **Animals down to birds and one herd kind.**
